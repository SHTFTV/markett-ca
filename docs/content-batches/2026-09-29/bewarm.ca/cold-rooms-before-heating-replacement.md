# Cold Rooms? What to Record Before Replacing Your Heating System

When one part of a home feels cold, replacing the heating appliance may not answer the whole problem. The building envelope, heat distribution and controls all deserve consideration. A short observation record helps a qualified professional investigate without forcing you to guess at a repair.

## Make a room-by-room comfort record

For several ordinary days, note the room, approximate time and whether the heating system was operating. Record whether doors were open or closed and whether the issue changed with windy or colder weather. If you have a room thermometer, use it consistently and keep it away from direct sun or a supply register. Treat readings as observations, not a professional heat-loss calculation.

Include rooms that become too warm. A useful assessment needs the complete pattern, not only the coldest spot. Also note unusual changes: a problem that began after a renovation or equipment replacement gives the assessor a place to start.

## Collect the home's history

Prepare a brief list of insulation upgrades, window replacements, additions and changes to how rooms are used. Bring equipment model numbers and past service records if readily available. Utility bills can provide context, but changes in occupancy, weather or rates can affect comparisons. Avoid assuming that a higher bill proves a particular component has failed.

## Ask whether an energy evaluation would help

BC Hydro describes an EnerGuide home evaluation as an assessment of factors such as air leakage, insulation, furnace and ductwork. It can help prioritize improvements across the home. An evaluation and a heating service call answer different questions: the former considers overall efficiency, while the latter can assess equipment operation. Ask which assessment best fits your concerns and whether both would be useful.

## Sequence the work before choosing equipment

If you plan substantial insulation or air-sealing work, tell the heating designer before equipment is selected. Natural Resources Canada notes that addressing building issues can reduce the heat-pump capacity needed. Ask the designer to explain whether the proposed sizing reflects the home as it is today or after planned improvements.

Use these questions at the appointment:

- What measurements would help distinguish an envelope issue from a distribution issue?
- Which improvements should happen before new equipment is selected?
- Will proposed changes affect ventilation or combustion-appliance operation?
- How will we check whether the comfort problem is resolved?

## Keep observation separate from repair

Do not open combustion equipment, modify gas piping or block ventilation to test a theory. FortisBC advises keeping appliance areas and vents clear and using qualified contractors for gas-appliance service. Follow your equipment manual for ordinary owner care. A suspected gas leak or carbon monoxide alarm is an emergency, not a comfort investigation: leave for a safe location and contact emergency services or the utility.

Bring your room notes, renovation history and questions to the assessment. That makes the discussion more specific and gives you a better basis for deciding which work to do first.
